Executive Summary

Counties with higher rates of household food insecurity experience systematically higher rates of diagnosed adult diabetes across the United States. Community food insecurity accounts for approximately 59.6% of the overall geographic variance in county adult diabetes rates nationwide (r = 0.7723).

Background

Managing chronic metabolic conditions like Type 2 diabetes requires predictable access to nutrient-dense foods and consistent care management. When households encounter severe nutritional insecurity, reliance on low-cost, shelf-stable, energy-dense foods increases, which can exacerbate glycemic volatility and metabolic disease progression.

To assist TruBridge leadership and regional health administrators in identifying high-priority resource allocation points, this brief evaluates public health records from the CDC Local Data for Better Health initiative combined with USDA Economic Research Service food insecurity metrics across 2,300 U.S. counties.

Methods

I merged county-level estimates of adult diabetes prevalence with household food insecurity percentages across 2,300 non-overlapping U.S. counties, and categorized counties into three risk quantiles (Low, Moderate, High) and conducted linear regression to quantify spatial co-occurrence patterns.

Methodological Limitation: This analysis utilizes population-level observational data rather than individual patient clinical records; consequently, findings describe community risk environments and cannot prove direct biological causality for individual patient outcomes.

Key Findings & Regional Breakdown

The linear trend demonstrates a strong, positive spatial co-occurrence: as county-level food insecurity increases, adult diabetes prevalence rises systematically. High-risk food insecurity counties average a diabetes prevalence of 16.06%, compared to 11.70% in low-risk counties. Southern states such as, Mississippi (17.25% diabetes, 25.83% food insecurity), West Virginia (16.98%), and Alabama (16.80%), represent the highest cumulative regional burden.

Practical Implications

Targeted Preventive Outreach

Population health teams might consider prioritizing mobile clinical units and chronic care management resources in counties where food insecurity exceeds 15%.

Integrated Intake Screenings

Care coordinators could evaluate incorporating routine SDOH food security screeners during patient intake to connect at-risk individuals with community programs.

Food Prescriptions & Partnerships

Healthcare administrators may want to explore partnerships with regional food banks to establish co-located produce prescription programs in high-burden counties like Mississippi and West Virginia.
